ICH E10 GUIDELINE: CHOICE OF CONTROL GROUP AND RELATED ISSUES IN CLINICAL TRIALS
Effective Date: July 27, 2001
Regulatory Body: ICH (International Council for Harmonisation)

SECTION 1: INTRODUCTION

The choice of control group is one of the most important design decisions in a clinical trial. It directly determines what question the trial is designed to answer and what evidence it will generate. This guideline discusses the types of control groups used in clinical trials, the scientific and ethical considerations for choosing among them, and the circumstances under which each type is appropriate.

SECTION 2: TYPES OF CONTROLS

2.1 Placebo Control
A placebo-controlled trial compares the experimental treatment with a control that is visually indistinguishable but contains no active substance. Placebo controls provide an unbiased estimate of the absolute effect of the experimental treatment. They are particularly valuable when: (a) no established therapy exists, (b) adding a placebo to standard therapy allows measurement of the incremental benefit of the experimental treatment, or (c) the condition is self-limiting and background variation is high.

Ethical acceptability of placebo controls depends on the severity of the condition and the availability of effective treatments. Placebo controls are ethically acceptable when no proven effective treatment exists, when withholding existing treatment would not cause serious irreversible harm, or when subjects have been offered and declined available treatment.

2.2 Active Control: Superiority Design
An active-controlled superiority trial tests whether the experimental treatment is better than an established treatment. This design answers the question: Is the new treatment superior to the existing standard? The trial requires the same rigor as a placebo-controlled trial and must be powered to detect the expected superiority margin.

2.3 Active Control: Non-Inferiority Design
A non-inferiority trial tests whether the experimental treatment is not unacceptably worse than an established active control. The clinical objective is to show that the new treatment, while potentially similar to the control in efficacy, offers advantages in another dimension (e.g., safety, convenience, cost).

Non-inferiority trials require careful specification of the non-inferiority margin (M2), which must be based on the historical placebo-controlled effect size of the active control (M1). The fraction of the active control's established effect that is preserved by the new treatment must be clinically and statistically justified. The concept of assay sensitivity is critical: the trial must be capable of detecting a difference between active and placebo if one exists.

2.4 Dose-Response Control
Dose-response designs use multiple doses of the experimental treatment as comparators. These designs are valuable in early Phase 2 to characterize the dose-response relationship and support dose selection for confirmatory trials.

2.5 No-Treatment Control
A no-treatment control (open comparison without placebo) may be appropriate when blinding is not feasible. However, the absence of blinding introduces observation bias, particularly for subjective endpoints. The potential for bias must be carefully considered.

SECTION 3: ASSAY SENSITIVITY

3.1 Definition and Importance
Assay sensitivity is the ability of a trial to distinguish an effective from an ineffective treatment. A trial lacks assay sensitivity if it would fail to detect a treatment difference even when one exists. In non-inferiority trials, lack of assay sensitivity makes the results uninterpretable: a non-inferiority finding in a trial that cannot differentiate effective from ineffective treatments is meaningless.

3.2 Historical Evidence Basis
Assay sensitivity in an active-controlled non-inferiority trial depends on the historical evidence that the active control is efficacious in the indication and the consistency of effect across historical trials and the current trial setting. If the active control's historical effect is uncertain or variable, the basis for the non-inferiority margin is unreliable.

3.3 Constancy Assumption
Non-inferiority trials rely on the constancy assumption: that the effect of the active control relative to placebo in the current trial is the same as in the historical trials used to establish the non-inferiority margin. Differences in patient population, standard of care, endpoint measurement, or background therapy between the historical and current settings can violate this assumption.

SECTION 4: THE NON-INFERIORITY MARGIN

4.1 Selection of M1
M1 is the entire effect of the active control compared to placebo as established from historical data. It represents the upper bound of what could be lost if the new treatment is identical to placebo. M1 should be estimated as the lower confidence bound of the active control's historical effect to provide a conservative estimate.

4.2 Selection of M2
M2 is the largest clinically acceptable difference by which the experimental treatment may be inferior to the active control. M2 must be less than M1 to ensure the trial provides evidence that the new treatment is better than placebo. The choice of M2 must consider the clinical consequences of the inferiority and the benefit of any non-efficacy advantages.

4.3 Pre-Specification
The non-inferiority margin must be pre-specified before unblinded analysis. Post-hoc specification of the non-inferiority margin undermines the confirmatory nature of the trial and will not be accepted by regulatory reviewers.

SECTION 5: CONSIDERATIONS FOR SPECIFIC THERAPEUTIC AREAS

5.1 Life-Threatening Conditions
For conditions with high mortality or severe morbidity, both scientific and ethical considerations argue against placebo controls when effective therapy exists. Active-controlled non-inferiority trials or add-on designs are preferred.

5.2 Chronic Conditions
In chronic conditions, long-term placebo control may be ethically acceptable when the condition, while uncomfortable, does not cause permanent harm and when patients are fully informed. The duration of placebo exposure must be justified.

5.3 Oncology
In oncology, placebo controls are typically acceptable for add-on designs (experimental treatment added to standard of care) but not for standalone placebo vs. active treatment when effective therapy exists and withholding it would compromise patient survival.

SECTION 6: CHOICE OF CONTROL: SCIENTIFIC CRITERIA

The scientifically most appropriate control group is the one that allows the trial to answer the clinical question with minimal bias and maximum precision. Key scientific considerations include: the ability to blind treatment assignment, the expected magnitude of placebo response, the variability in outcomes, and the feasibility of measuring the primary endpoint reliably.

Whichever control group is chosen, the rationale must be explicitly documented in the protocol. Regulatory submissions must include a clear explanation of why the chosen control group is appropriate for the indication, the patient population, and the clinical development stage.
