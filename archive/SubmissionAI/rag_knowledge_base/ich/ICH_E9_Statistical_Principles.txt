ICH E9 GUIDELINE: STATISTICAL PRINCIPLES FOR CLINICAL TRIALS
Effective Date: September 1, 1998
Regulatory Body: ICH (International Council for Harmonisation)

SECTION 1: INTRODUCTION

This guideline addresses the principles of statistical methodology applied to clinical trials for purposes of regulatory submissions. It is intended to assist sponsors in designing and analyzing trials that generate valid evidence of drug efficacy and safety and to assist regulatory authorities in evaluating such evidence. The guideline applies to Phase 2 and Phase 3 confirmatory clinical trials and to any other trials whose results are intended to support regulatory decisions.

SECTION 2: CONSIDERATIONS FOR OVERALL CLINICAL DEVELOPMENT

2.1 Role of Statistics in Clinical Trials
Statistics provides the scientific basis for planning, conducting, analyzing, and evaluating clinical trials. The statistical framework must align with the clinical objectives of the trial. The main roles of statistics are to help ensure that clinical trials are properly planned to answer the clinical question with appropriate certainty, and to provide quantitative estimates of treatment effects with valid confidence intervals.

2.2 Scope of Confirmatory and Exploratory Trials
Confirmatory trials are pre-planned tests of pre-specified hypotheses. Their primary purpose is to provide firm evidence of a causal relationship between treatment and outcome. Exploratory trials generate hypotheses that may subsequently be tested in confirmatory trials. The distinction is important: exploratory analyses from confirmatory trials must not be presented as confirmatory evidence.

SECTION 3: STUDY DESIGN CONSIDERATIONS

3.1 Design Configuration
The study design must be appropriate for the objectives. Parallel group designs are common for chronic conditions. Crossover designs are appropriate when carryover effects are absent. The design must specify the comparison type: superiority, non-inferiority, or equivalence.

3.2 Sample Size
Sample size must be determined prospectively. The determination requires specification of: the primary endpoint and its expected distribution, the clinically relevant treatment difference (effect size), the Type I error (alpha), the power (1 - beta), and the statistical test to be used. If multiple primary endpoints exist, the sample size must account for the multiple testing procedure. Any assumptions used in the sample size calculation must be justified and, where possible, based on observed data from prior trials.

3.3 Randomization
Randomization is the primary tool to prevent selection bias and to provide the basis for statistical inference. The randomization schedule must be generated by a well-defined, reproducible process and must be concealed from investigators at the time of allocation. Dynamic allocation methods (e.g., minimization) should be described in sufficient statistical detail to allow replication.

3.4 Stratification
Stratification at randomization ensures balance of key prognostic factors between treatment groups and increases statistical precision. Stratification factors should be pre-specified and their use in the primary analysis pre-planned. Ignoring stratification factors in the analysis when they were used in the randomization can introduce bias and loss of power.

3.5 Blinding
Double-blind studies are preferred for confirmatory trials to prevent bias in outcome assessment. When blinding is not possible, the rationale must be provided and measures taken to reduce bias must be described. Any unplanned unblinding must be reported and the potential impact on trial integrity assessed.

SECTION 4: TRIAL CONDUCT CONSIDERATIONS

4.1 Protocol Deviations
The protocol must specify which deviations are critical and may exclude subjects from the per-protocol analysis. A deviation management plan should be in place prior to unblinding. Systematic protocol deviations (those affecting many subjects or related to treatment) are particularly concerning and must be reported and analyzed for impact.

4.2 Adherence to Randomized Treatment
The proportion of subjects who complete treatment per protocol is an important quality indicator. Premature discontinuation is often non-random and may bias the results. The reasons for discontinuation must be analyzed for potential associations with treatment assignment or outcomes.

SECTION 5: STATISTICAL ANALYSIS PLAN (SAP)

5.1 SAP Pre-Specification Requirements
The Statistical Analysis Plan is the document that defines all analyses to be conducted on the clinical trial data. The SAP must be finalized before database lock and before any unblinded data are reviewed. Retrospective changes to the SAP after database lock require written justification and will be considered potential sources of bias by regulatory reviewers.

5.2 Contents of the SAP
The SAP must include: the primary analysis method with all assumptions, secondary endpoint analyses, analysis populations (ITT, mITT, PP, safety), covariate adjustment methodology, subgroup analyses, interim analysis plan with alpha spending, missing data handling, and sensitivity analyses. All tables, figures, and listings shells should be finalized before database lock.

5.3 Analysis Populations
The intent-to-treat (ITT) principle specifies that all randomized subjects should be analyzed in the group to which they were randomized regardless of whether they received treatment or complied with the protocol. An ITT analysis provides an unbiased estimate of the treatment effect in the population intended to be treated. A per-protocol (PP) population excludes subjects with major protocol deviations. Both analyses should be conducted and compared. Meaningful differences between ITT and PP results require investigation.

SECTION 6: MISSING DATA AND HANDLING

6.1 Impact of Missing Data
Missing data are an almost universal problem in clinical trials and can introduce bias that undermines the validity of results. The mechanism of missingness (Missing Completely at Random [MCAR], Missing at Random [MAR], or Missing Not at Random [MNAR]) determines the appropriate analytical approach.

6.2 Missing Data Methods
Single imputation methods such as Last Observation Carried Forward (LOCF) or Non-Responder Imputation (NRI) make strong assumptions that are often violated. NRI is appropriate for response endpoints when clinical assumptions support treating dropouts as non-responders. LOCF assumes the outcome does not change after dropout, which is often implausible.

Multiple imputation under a MAR assumption is more defensible when the missing data mechanism can be reasonably assumed to be MAR conditional on observed covariates. Pattern-mixture models and shared-parameter models address MNAR assumptions. At least one sensitivity analysis addressing a plausible departure from the primary missing data assumption is strongly recommended.

6.3 Sensitivity Analyses for Missing Data
Sensitivity analyses should stress-test the primary missing data assumption. Tipping-point analyses, delta-adjustment methods, and reference-based imputation are valuable tools. The chosen sensitivity analyses must be pre-specified and justified.

SECTION 7: EVALUATION OF SAFETY AND TOLERABILITY

Safety analyses must include all subjects who received at least one dose of study medication. Adverse event incidence rates, severity, causality, and discontinuation due to adverse events must be tabulated. Statistical testing of safety endpoints is generally exploratory because safety studies are rarely powered for formal inference. However, pre-specified safety endpoints of particular clinical importance may be analyzed with pre-specified statistical tests.

SECTION 8: MULTIPLICITY

8.1 The Multiplicity Problem
When multiple hypotheses are tested, the probability that at least one test will yield a false-positive result increases with the number of tests. Multiplicity concerns arise from multiple primary endpoints, multiple treatment comparisons, multiple timepoints, and interim analyses.

8.2 Control of Type I Error
Procedures to control the family-wise error rate (FWER) at alpha include the Bonferroni correction, the Holm step-down procedure, and hierarchical (gatekeeping) testing. Hierarchical testing is commonly used in trials with a primary endpoint and sequentially tested secondary endpoints. Secondary endpoints are only tested if the primary endpoint achieves statistical significance, thereby protecting the overall Type I error rate.

8.3 Multiple Endpoints
When multiple co-primary endpoints are used, all must achieve statistical significance. When a primary endpoint and secondary endpoints are tested, the testing order and procedure must be pre-specified. Confirmatory interpretations of secondary endpoints require pre-specification of the analysis and correction for multiplicity.

SECTION 9: EVALUATION OF ESTIMATIONS

Beyond hypothesis testing, estimation of the treatment effect with appropriate confidence intervals is essential. The confidence interval conveys both the magnitude and precision of the estimated treatment effect. Clinical significance must be interpreted in relation to the minimum clinically important difference, not solely on the basis of statistical significance.

SECTION 10: REPORTING OF RESULTS

All analyses specified in the SAP must be reported regardless of whether results are statistically significant. Selective reporting of favorable results or failure to report pre-specified analyses are serious departures from scientific integrity. Post-hoc analyses must be clearly labeled and interpreted as hypothesis-generating. The report must clearly distinguish between confirmatory and exploratory findings.
